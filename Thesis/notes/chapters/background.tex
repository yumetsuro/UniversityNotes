\section{The Inherent Dynamism of Natural Systems}
Dynamics are an intrinsic aspect of reality, and the universe can be thought of as a complex, non-linear dynamical system where the state of any part is constantly
changing over time due to interactions with other parts.
Historically, the mathematical modelling of such systems began with the study of simple differential equations as a method
to solve the two-body problems, but many natural phenomena are essentially Complex Systems (CS), emerging from simple interactions between single components of the 
environment, which exhibits complex dynamics that evolve through time:
such as motion of planets, flow of fluids, weather changes, growth of population \cite{renshaw1993modelling} and many other phenomena.\newline

Since dynamics is such an important aspect of natural systems, many real world processes can be modelled as dynamical systems,
eg. the design of a control system, the stock market \cite{10.5555/322494} and also the human brain \cite{RevModPhys.78.1213} can be modeled as a dynamical system.\newline
Troughout the last century the analysis of sequential data, commonly referred as \textit{time series}, became increasingly critical for understanding dynamical behaviours. Consequently specific methodological frameworks were developed to model such data.
The approaches encompass techniques such as Autoregressive (AR) 
models which employ linear regression on data sequences,
extending to more sophisticasted frameworks including State-Space Models \cite{kalman1960new}, originally developed for Control Theory applications,
 and Hidden Markov Models (HMM) \cite{baum1966statistical}. The latter served as fundational precursors to Recurrent Neural Networks \cite{ELMAN1990179} (RNNs).
 The aforementioned models are the basis of modern neural-based sequential modeling.\newline


\section{Dynamical Systems review}
A dynamical system is a system which evolves over time we can describe it's changes throught \textbf{state variables}, usually many models are constructed such that
only the foundamental state variables are considered, for example \textit{biological spiking neurons} modeled as in \cite{1257420} are described by a simple 2-D dimensional ODE, where the two state variables are the membrane potential and a recovery variable.
ignoring existing other possible variables, such gradient of ion concentration, temperature, for the sake of obtaining a large scale simulation.\newline

There are two types of dynamical systems: \textit{Differential Equations}, which are continuous in time,
and Difference Equations or \textit{Iterated Maps}, which are discrete in time. 
The most used in a wide range of scientific fields are the former, which are further distinguished in Ordinary Differential Equations (ODE) 
and Partial Differential Equations (PDE), the difference is that in PDE we have two or more independent variables.  \newline

This work will treat ODEs in their discretized form which we'll see in section \ref{sec:discretization_methods} to obtain it's approximated solution.\newline
 Formally, we define an ODE as:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = f(\mathbf{x},t)
    \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the state vector, $t$ is the time and $f:\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is a
vector field grouping all the state variables that describe the evolution of the system. \newline

A Differential Equation is \textit{autonomous} if the function $f$ does not depend explicitly on the independent variable, such as time; otherwise it is \textit{non-autonomous}.
We can switch from a non-autonomous to an autonomous system by adding the independent variable as a new variable to the state vector, for example, the
non-autonomous system of equation \ref{eq:armonic} can be rewritten with a change of variable.

Then the solution of the ODE is called \textit{initial value problem}.\newline

\end{definition}
In order to solve the IVP, we need to find a function $\mathbf{x}(t)$ that satisfies both the differential equation and the initial condition.

\subsection{Stability}\todo{forse è meglio usare un immagine invece di scrivere tutto questo formalismo}
Stability \cite{strogatz:2000} is a fundamental concept in the study of dynamical systems; it describes how a system responds to small perturbations or changes in initial conditions.

A system is said to be \textbf{stable} if small perturbations in the initial conditions lead to small changes in the system's behaviour over time.
On the other hand, a system is \textbf{unstable} if small perturbations can lead to large deviations in behaviour.
\newline
Considering the generic equation \ref{eq:ode}, an equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}^*$ is a point where $f(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0$. The stability of this equilibrium point can be analysed using the following definitions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lyapunov Stability}: An equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}^*$ is Lyapunov stable if, for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that if $||\mathbf{x}(t_0) - \mathbf{x}^*|| < \delta$, 
    then $||\mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}^*|| < \epsilon$ for all $t \geq t_0$. The system remains close to the equilibrium point remains close to the equilibrium point for all future times within the $\epsilon$ bound. 
    
    \item \textbf{Asymptotic Stability}: An equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}^*$ is asymptotically stable if it is Lyapunov stable and, 
    in addition, there exists a $\delta' > 0$ such that if $||\mathbf{x}(t_0) - \mathbf{x}^*|| < \delta'$, then $\lim_{t \to \infty} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{x}^*$. The system remains close to the equilibrium point, but it also converges to it over time.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Stability}: An equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}^*$ is uniformly stable if the $\delta$ in the Lyapunov stability definition can be chosen independently of the initial time $t_0$.
    \item \textbf{Instability}: An equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}^*$ is unstable if it is not Lyapunov stable.
\end{itemize}

To analyse the stability of an equilibrium point, we can use linearization, which involves approximating the system near the equilibrium point using its Jacobian matrix.
The Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \bigg|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^*}
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix provide insights into the local behaviour of the system near the equilibrium point.

\subsection{Discretization Methods}\label{sec:discretization_methods} \todo{quanto in dettaglio?}    
Finding an analytical solution for an ODE is unfeasible in many cases; thus, we need to rely on numerical methods
to approximate the solution. Discretising the time variables in small steps of size $\tau$, there are many ways to approximate the solution of an ODE,
but two main classes exist: \textbf{Explicit} and \textbf{Implicit} methods.\newline
Implicit methods are more stable and accurate for numerically unstable equations (also known as stiff systems\footnote{Stiff systems are those where some terms change very rapidly compared to others.}), but require solving a system of equations at each timestep, which can be computationally expensive. On the other hand,
Explicit methods compute the state at the timestep $t+1$ directly from the current state at time $t$, for example the well known \textbf{Euler's method} \cite{scott2011numerical} approximates the solution as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(t+\tau) = \mathbf{x}(t) + \tau f(\mathbf{x}(t), t)
\end{equation}

The main methods used for this work are the Explicit Euler's method, which introduces some error, the local discretisation error,
is $||\mathbf{x}(t+\tau) - \mathbf{x}_{\text{approx}}(t+\tau)|| = O(\tau^2)$, while the global error over $N$ steps is $O(\tau)$. 
This method is preferred since we're trying to 
lowering computation cost as much as possible, however, in order to keep the computation stable, we need to use small time steps $\tau$.
Of course exists a wide range of more advanced methods, such as the \textbf{Runge-Kutta} methods or even Adaptive Runge-Kutta \cite{scott2011numerical} which provide higher order accuracy if needed.

\section{Overview of Machine Learning}
Machine Learning (ML) is a subfield of Artificial Intelligence (AI) that focuses on statistical methods and algorithms capable of \textit{learn} from past experiences.
First, we need a rigorous definition of what it means to learn; \textcite[]{mitchell1997machine} defines it as: "A computer program is said to \textbf{learn} from experience $\mathbf{E}$ with respect to 
some class of tasks $\mathbf{T}$ and performance measure $\mathbf{P}$, if its performance at tasks in $\mathbf{T}$, as measured by $\mathbf{P}$, improves with experience $\mathbf{E}$."

ML can tackle a much wider range of tasks that are difficult to solve with explicit rules-based programming; in a certain sense, it bypasses
some level of abstraction, we're not learning the task $T$ directly, but we're sampling from the $E$ space, \textbf{examples}, which are 
collection of \textbf{features} that are qualitative or quantitative properties of the data that are exploited by the model to learn the task $T$.%\newline
\subsection{The learning problem}
%Let's start with a definition
A formal definition of a machine learning model is given below:

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ be the input space, and let
$p_{\text{data}}$ denote an unknown data-generating distribution over $\mathcal{X}$.
We assume that the dataset consists of $N$ independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d.) samples drawn from $p_{\text{data}}$, namely
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{dev}} = \{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{N},
\qquad \mathbf{x}_i \sim p_{\text{data}}.
\]
Let $\mathcal{Y}$ denote the output (or target) space.
A \textbf{machine learning model} is a family of prediction functions
\[
f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y},
\]
parameterized by $\theta \in \Theta$.
The nature of the model and its learning objective are determined by the
chosen learning paradigm:
\end{definition}

\begin{itemize}
    \item In the \textbf{supervised} setting, the data is drawn from a joint distribution $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}, y)$, 
    so $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{k}$, with targets $y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$. 
    Here, $\mathcal{Y}$ (e.g., $\mathbb{R}$ for regression or $\{0,1\}$ for classification), 
    and the goal is to find parameters $\theta^*$ that minimize a loss function $\mathcal{L}(y, f(\mathbf{x}; \theta))$ 
    quantifying the discrepancy between predictions and true labels.

    \item In the \textbf{unsupervised} setting, the data consists only of inputs $\mathbf{x}_i$. 
    The output space $\mathcal{Y}$ is defined by the specific task (e.g., $\mathbb{R}^k$ for dimensionality reduction, 
    a set of clusters for clustering, or $\mathbb{R}^d$ for generative models). The goal is to find parameters $\theta^*$
     that optimize an objective function $\mathcal{J}(f(\mathbf{x}; \theta), \mathcal{D})$,
      which captures desired structural properties of the data, such as reconstruction error, cluster cohesion, or data likelihood.
\end{itemize}
In both cases, the ultimate objective is for the model $f(\mathbf{x}; \theta^*)$ to generalize well to unseen data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}} \sim P(\mathbf{x})$. 

The model $f_{\theta}$\footnote{We will use $f_{\theta}$ as shorthand for $f(\mathbf{x}; \theta)$}  is said to belong to a \textbf{hypothesis space} $\mathcal{H}$, 
which is the set of all possible functions the model can represent by varying its parameters $\theta$.
The choice of model architecture (e.g., linear models, neural networks, decision trees) \cite{lecun2015deep}
defines the hypothesis space. A larger, more expressive $\mathcal{H}$ can represent more complex functions.
The learning algorithm searches within $\mathcal{H}$ to find the hypothesis 
$f^* = f(\cdot; \theta^*)$ that best approximates the true underlying relationship between inputs and outputs.

\subsection{Modelling approaches: Generative vs Discriminative}
Both supervised and unsupervised models differ in how they represent data distributions.
There are two main approaches: \textbf{Generative} and \textbf{Discriminative} learning.\newline
Discriminative models learn the conditional probability $p(y|\mathbf{x})$ directly, focusing on the decision boundary between classes. They are typically used for classification and regression tasks.

In contrast, generative models learn the joint probability distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$, modelling how the data is generated. So that they can generate samples that are similar to the training data, and are used for density estimation (e.g., Gaussian Mixture Models, Hidden Markov Models) \cite[Ch. 5.8]{lecun2015deep}, unsupervised learning, and semi-supervised learning.

in practice what is often adopted is called the \textit{empirical} approach based on risk minimization

We seek a function $f_{\theta}$ that minimizes the expected risk $\mathcal{R}(f_\theta)$ over $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}, y)$.
The loss function acts as a proxy for the learning process.
Risk has a dual nature: the expected risk and the empirical risk.

The \textbf{expected risk} (or population risk) of a hypothesis $f_\theta$ measures its

\subsubsection{Risk Decomposition}

%We choose $f_{\theta}$ from $\mathcal{H}$  such that the expected risk can be decomposed as:
Most of the time, the space of possible solutions is unfathomably large; thus, we need to decompose the risk in order to understand if 
we are generalizing correctly.\newline
Let $f_{\mathcal{H}}^* = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \mathcal{R}(f)$ be the best possible function within the hypothesis space, 
and let $\hat f_{\mathcal{H}} = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \hat{\mathcal{R}}(f)$ denote the empirical risk minimizer found by the learning algorithm.
Then, the population risk of the learned model can be decomposed in: \cite{10.5555/3360093}\cite{Vapnik1998}\cite{mjt_dlt}\cite[pag. 33, Ch 2]{bach2024learning}
%\cite{mjt_dlt}\cite[pag. 33 Ch 2]{bach2024learning}
%\cite[pag. 168, Ch 12.3]{books/daglib/0033642}

%The \textbf{approximation term} reflects the intrinsic limitations of the hypothesis space~$\mathcal{H}$, 
%the \textbf{generalization term} measures the difference between empirical and true performance, 
%and the \textbf{optimization term} accounts for the gap due to imperfect minimization of the empirical risk.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:risk_decomposition}
    \mathcal{R}(f_\theta) = 
    \underbrace{\mathcal{R}(f_\theta) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}(f_\theta)}_{\text{Generalization error}}
     + \underbrace{\hat{\mathcal{R}}(f_\theta) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}({f^*}_\mathcal{H})}_{\text{Optimization error}} 
     + \underbrace{\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{f}_\mathcal{H}) - \mathcal{R}(f_\mathcal{H}^*)}_{\text{Estimation error}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{R}(f_\mathcal{H}^*)}_{\text{Approximation error}} 
\end{equation}

The approximation error is irreducible because the data-generating process includes 
an intrinsic noise~$\varepsilon$ that cannot be captured by any hypothesis
 in~$\mathcal{H}$. Consequently, learning algorithms must focus on reducing the remaining
  components. When the selected hypothesis~$f_\theta$ is insufficiently 
  expressive, \textbf{underfitting} occurs: the empirical risk $\hat{\mathcal{R}}(f_\theta)$ may be small,
   yet the estimated hypothesis fails to approximate the target function.\footnote{In the case of limited data availability}
   If $f_\theta$ possesses excessive capacity, the estimator
     can \textit{interpolate} $\mathcal{D}_{train}$, attaining negligible empirical risk, but with a large generalization gap; 
     this phenomenon is called \textbf{overfitting}.

However, learning differs from pure optimization, ML methods optimize indirectly with respect to the population risk.

The typical ML objective function minimized is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \mathcal{D}_{train}}[\mathcal{L}(y, f(\mathbf{x}; \theta))] + \lambda \Omega(\mathbf{w}) 
\end{equation}

The term $\Omega(\mathbf{w})$ is a regularization term that allows to control the complexity of the model, $\lambda$
is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of the regularization.\newline
Regularization can be done in different ways, for example, using a prior distribution over the parameters, or in optimization, we can penalize with different 
norms, for example, $L2$ or $L1$ norms, if we are interested in decreasing the magnitude of parameters or inducing sparsity, respectively.\newline
The algorithm that empowers the entire ML is \textbf{Stochastic Gradient Descent} (SGD) \cite[pag. 152, Ch 5.2]{Goodfellow-et-al-2016} 
which iteratively updates the parameters $\theta$ in the opposite direction of the gradient of the loss function with respect to the parameters.
Typically, the parameters are initialised in some configuration $\theta_0$, a good initialization is often crucial to convergence
 and at each iteration $t$, we compute the gradient of the loss function
with respect to the parameters $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$, then we update the parameters using a learning rate $\eta$ that controls the step size or magnitude of update, and usually is one 
the most influential factors that determine how quickly and accurately the model converges in a region with an optimal solution.
\newline
The learning rule is written as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:learning_rule}
    \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)
\end{equation}
More advanced optimization algorithms exist, such as \textbf{Adam} \cite{kingma2017adammethodstochasticoptimization} but are based on SGD with some modifications.
Moreover, SGD can be performed in either
online, mini-batch, or full-batch mode.
In the online setting, the learning rule in \eqref{eq:learning_rule} is
applied using a single training example at a time.
In contrast, mini-batch and full-batch SGD compute parameter updates using,
respectively, a subset or the entirety of the training dataset
$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$.
Mini-batch training is generally preferred when $|\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}|$
is large, as processing the full dataset at each update step may be
computationally infeasible or exceed available memory.
Full-batch optimization is instead practical when the dataset is sufficiently
small to fit entirely in memory.
Mini-batch optimization introduces
stochasticity into the parameter updates, resulting in noisy gradient
estimates.
This noise can be beneficial, as it may help the optimization process escape
poor local minima or saddle points.
At the same time, the expected update direction of mini-batch gradients
approximates the true gradient of the empirical risk, as discussed
in \cite{WILSON20031429}.
\subsubsection{Model selection and Hyperparameters}
The standard pipeline for training a machine learning model follows a systematic approach.
 Given a specific task and
a sampled dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{dev}} \sim p_{\text{data}}$ partitioned into three subsets: 
training $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, validation $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$ , and test sets $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$. The training set optimizes model parameters, 
the validation set serves for hyperparameter tuning and architectural decisions,
 and the test set provides an unbiased performance estimate.
Hyperparameters are those parameters not learned during training but set prior to the learning process. 
We have seen examples like the regularization coefficient $\lambda$ and the learning rate $\eta$.
The training phase primarily reduces the estimation error $\mathcal{R}(f_\theta) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}(f_\theta)$, from
eq. \ref{eq:risk_decomposition}. Model selection, controls the approximation error $\mathcal{R}(f_\mathcal{H}^*)$
 by determining the expressiveness of $\mathcal{H}$ trough hyperparameter choices.  
Model selection involves training multiple candidate models with varying architectures and 
hyperparameters on the training set, then selecting the configuration with the best validation performance. 
Validation methods, such as K-fold cross-validation or Bootstrapping \cite{lecun2015deep} and Bayesian optimization search \cite{8791696} are often used
in order to search more efficiently in the space of possible models.

\section{Neural Networks and Deep Learning}
Neural networks (NNs) \cite[Ch. 3.1]{prince2023understanding} are flexible non-linear universal function approximators \cite{cybenko1989approximation} \cite{bach2024learning}
which are inspired by the biological neural system, where information is processed in hierarchical manner. 
In fact, the idea originates from the layerwise brain organization in the visual cortex
where information is processed at different complexity scales as discussed in \cite{fukushima1980neocognitron}. 
Specifically, a shallow feedforward neural network (FFN) is composed of interconnected nodes called \textit{perceptrons} \cite{rosenblatt1958perceptron} or artificial neurons divided into:
input layer, that receives the perceived signal $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, one hidden layer, and an output layer that predicts the target $\hat{y}$.
By stacking hidden layers we are composing together many different functions eg. $f_{net}^l \circ f_{net}^{l-1} \circ ... \circ f_{net}^1$, with each hidden layer having its own parameters:  $\theta = \left\{ {W^{(l)}}, \mathbf{b^{(l)}} \right\}}$,
 increasing the complexity of the model as well as the hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$.

This is the main idea behind \textbf{Deep Learning} \cite[Ch 4.3]{prince2023understanding}, which refers to Deep neural networks (DNNs) which are the core 
of many state-of-the-art machine learning systems \cite{lecun2015deep}.
\newline
Consider $f_{net}^l$ to be the generic output at layer $l$ of a DNN, composed by two parts: an affine transformation $W$, followed by a non-linear activation or transfer function
$\sigma$. \newline
\begin{equation}
    f_{net}^l(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(W^{(l)}\mathbf{x} + b^{(l)}) = \sigma(\sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i^{(l)} x_i + b^{(l)})\footnote{With $x_i$ and $w_i^{(l)}$ the $i$-th element of $\mathbf{x}$ and $W^{(l)}$ respectively}
\end{equation}
We call $W^{(l)}$ the weight matrix, $\mathbf{b}^{(l)}$ the bias vector, $\sigma$ is an arbitrary\footnote{While for hidden layers the right activation is usually chosen by model selection, for the output layer the activation regards the task
e.g., identity for regression, logistic for binary classification, and softmax for multiclassifiers} non-linear function such as ReLU, Sigmoid or Tanh.
$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector.
The parameters of this model, weight, are estimated through an algorithm known as \textbf{Backpropagation} \cite{rumelhart1986learning},
 which is an application of the chain rule of calculus to compute the gradient of the loss function with respect to the model parameters.


\subsection{Recurrent Neural Networks for Sequential Data}
% cite the unreasonably effectivenes of RNN...
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) \cite{rumelhart1986learning} are a class of neural networks designed to handle sequential values.
Their recurrent architecture introduces an \textit{architectural bias} towards capturing sequential dependancies. Even prior to training, recurrent units can display clustering resembling Markovian dynamics \cite{1263574}.
\newline
 In contrast to FNNs, which process input samples
independently and assume a fixed-dimensional input representation, sequential
data are characterized by temporal dependencies and long-range contextual
structure.
As a consequence, the effective complexity of modeling sequential data grows
with the length of the sequence, i.e., with the number of time steps $T$.
 \newline
 \newline
 Formally, RNNs are factorizing the probability of a sequence into a product of conditional distributions over time \cite{sutskever2014sequencesequencelearningneural}. 
Rather than conditioning explicitly on the entire past sequence, RNNs introduce a hidden state that acts as a compact summary of previous observations. 
The probability of a sequence $\mathbf{x}_{1:T} = [\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T]$ is thus modeled as
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{h}_{t-1}; \theta),
\end{equation}
where the hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t$ is updated recursively according to
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_t = f(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t; \theta).
\end{equation}
Through this recursive update, the hidden state provides a finite-dimensional representation of the history $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}$, enabling the model to capture temporal dependencies without explicitly storing past inputs.
 \newline
\begin{comment}
Formally, RNNs model the joint distribution of a sequence $\mathbf{x}_{1:T} = [\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T]$, by factorizing it over $T$ steps
 as a product of conditional distributions \cite{sutskever2014sequencesequencelearningneural}:
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}; \theta)
\end{equation}
\end{comment}
 RNNs introduce parameter sharing across time steps, allowing them to generalize across different sequence lengths and capture temporal dependencies.
Parameters sharing embodies a key \textit{inductive bias}: the assumption of temporal stationarity.
This assumption implies that the same parameters are updated each time step, so that the conditional probability distribution over variables at time
$t+1$ given all previous time steps does not depend explicitly on $t$, but only on the hidden state at time $t$.\newline\newline
For any time indices $t,k \in \mathbb{N}$, transition and output distributions: $p(\mathbf{h}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{h}_{t}; \theta) = p(\mathbf{h}_{k+1} \mid \mathbf{h}_{k}; \theta)$ and 
$p(\mathbf{y}_{t} \mid \mathbf{h}_{t}; \theta) = p(\mathbf{y}_{k} \mid \mathbf{h}_{k}; \theta)$, share the same parameter set $\theta$ governing the dynamics at each time step, 
consequently, temporal dependencies are captured solely through the evolution of the hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t$, without explicit 
dependence on $t$.
\newline\newline
 Similar to seminal works as Time Delay Neural Networks (TDNNs) \cite{peddinti15b_interspeech}, 
 RNNs maintain a hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t$ that is updated at each time step based on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$.
\newline
Computational graphs are often used to represent the structure of RNNs. These are part of the family of structured probabilistic models or 
\textbf{Graphical Models} \cite{barberBRML2011}, i.e., Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), Bayesian Networks (BNs), and Conditional Random Fields (CRFs) \cite[Ch. 4]{barberBRML2011}.
Where nodes represent random variables, and edges represent dependencies between them, similarly, we can think 
to RNNs as a specialization of these probabilistic models, but with a parameterized approach to the dependencies.
\newline

% RNNs definitions
An RNNs equation at time step $t$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}(t) = \tanh(W_{in} \mathbf{x}(t) + W_{rec} \mathbf{h}(t-1) + \mathbf{b})
\end{equation}
Where $\mathbf{h}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_h}$ is the hidden state at time $t$, $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_x}$ is the input vector at time $t$,
$W_{in} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_h \times N_x}$ is the input weight matrix, $W_{rec} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_h \times N_h}$ is the recurrent weight matrix, $\mathbf{b} \in
    \mathbb{R}^{N_h}$ is the bias vector, and $\tanh$ is the hyperbolic tangent activation function applied element-wise.

The output $\mathbf{y}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_y}$ at time $t$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}(t) = W_{out} \mathbf{h}(t) +
    \mathbf{c}
\end{equation}

RNNs can be \textit{unrolled} through time, which results in a directed acyclic graph (DAG), which helps
visualizing the flow of information through such networks, as we can see from the figure \ref{fig:rnn_unrolled}.

\subsection{The challenge for Long-Term Dependencies}
\label{sec:rnn_long_term_dependencies}
RNNs are powerful models for sequential data modelling; however, in the last years, they have been largely replaced by Transformer architectures \cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}.
This happened mainly because of the difficulty in training RNNs to capture long-term dependencies in sequences \cite{article}, \cite{230622}, \cite{279181}. The problem is addressed as \textit{information latching} \cite{298725}, which
consists in the ability of such networks to retain a robust representation of bits of information, this happens when the states of the network are contained in a reduced attracting set of a hyperbolic attractor \cite{haykin2009neural}, in the neighbourhood of this set the derivatives vanish and hence cannot be propagated through time to reliably latch the set.
In this work, we will try to address this problem when proposing our solutions that are based on
previous literature on RNNs designed to capture long-term dependencies; we will briefly summarize the main issues and the solutions proposed in the literature.
\subsubsection{The cause of Vanishing and Exploding Gradients}
By looking again at picture \ref{fig:rnn_unrolled},
if we imagine a really long sequence, the gradients will be propagated back through many time steps, this can lead to numerical instability and make long-term dependencies hard to learn.
This is due to the repeated multiplication of the gradients by the Jacobian matrix of the hidden state with respect to itself at the previous time step.
Essentially, we compute the following loss:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial W_{rec}} = \sum_{k=1}^{t} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t} \left(\prod_{l=k}^{t-1} \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{l+1}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_l}\right) \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}{\partial W_{rec}}
\end{equation}
where $\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{l+1}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_l}$ is the Jacobian matrix at time step $l$. The product $\prod_{l=k}^{t-1} \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{l+1}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_l}$ grows exponentially with the sequence length $(t-k)$, 
leading to \textit{vanishing} or \textit{exploding} (V/E) gradients \footnote{Gradient issues is related to depth of a network, even sufficiently deep FFNs suffers of V/E issues.} \cite[pag. 290, Ch 8.2]{lecun2015deep} depending on the spectral properties of these Jacobian matrices.
Suppose that $W_{rec}$ eigendecomposes as $W_{rec}^t = V \text{diag}({\lambda})^t V^{-1}$.
Exploding happen when the $|\lambda_{max}| > 1$, while vanishing when $|\lambda_{max}| < 1$.
The Jacobian matrix provides information about how small changes in $h_t$ propagate to the next step, or equivalently, how the gradient on $h_{t+1}$ propagates at the previous step.
Moreover, the recurrent matrix and the Jacobian can have complex-valued eigenvalues, with an imaginary part that induces oscillatory dynamics, making more complicated dynamics easier to learn. 
\newline
Several solutions have been proposed in literature to mitigate this problem, such as gradient clipping  \cite{pmlr-v28-pascanu13},
residual connections \cite{he2015deepresiduallearningimage} that help by adding to the timesteps directly the signal from previous steps, as well approaches directly acting on the recurrent 
matrix that fixes its spectrum to 1, such as orthogonal or unitary RNNs \cite{pmlr-v48-arjovsky16} \cite{le2015simplewayinitializerecurrent}.
Even choosing the right transfer function can help: Sigmoidal activations tend to saturate very quickly since their derivatives approach $0$ in the extreme regions, alternatives such as $\tanh$ that
saturate less, or ReLU that has more stable gradients and regularize at the same time by sparsifying activations \cite{pmlr-v15-glorot11a} contribute to the architectural bias.
\newline
A fundamental insight into solving the vanishing gradient came from Hochreiter et al. \cite{article}, introducing the concept of \textbf{Constant Error Carousel} (CEC),
which allows gradients to flow unchanged through time in the backward pass by using self-loops with unit weight in the recurrent connections.
But with CEC, the error is integrated over time, and time-based tasks often require memory to change dynamically.
\subsubsection{Gated RNNs}
To address the memory issues of learning long-term dependencies in sequential data, 
specialized RNN architectures with gating mechanisms have been developed, specifically
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks \cite{article} and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) \cite{cho2014properties}.
Such architectures aimed to solve Neural Machine Translation (NMT) over long sentences, where standard RNNs failed due to saturation of their memory capabilities.
The gates in these models solve the CEC problem by allowing the network to learn selectively what information should be integrated or forgotten over time.
\newline
The \textit{forget gate} uses sigmoid activation to decide what amount of information to discard from the previous $h_t$, while
the \textit{input gate} controls how much of the new information from the current input $x_t$ should be added to the cell state.
Finally, the \textit{output gate} determines what part of the cell state should be output as the new hidden state $h_t$.

For LSTMs the equations governing the gates and cell state updates are as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        f_t &= \sigma(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(forget gate)}\\ 
        i_t &= \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(input gate)}\\
        o_t &= \sigma(W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(output gate)}\\
        \tilde{C}_t &= \tanh(W_C \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(cell candidate)}\\
        C_t &= f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t \;\; \text{(cell state update)}\\
        h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(C_t) \;\; \text{(hidden state update)}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Where $W_f, W_i, W_o, W_C$ are weight matrices for the respective gates and cell candidate.

Instead, the GRU architecture combines the forget and input gates into a single \textit{update gate}, simplifying the model while retaining similar capabilities. 
But having fewer parameters makes GRUs faster to train and less prone to overfitting on smaller datasets.
\textbf{Update gates} acts as a conditional leaky integrator, deciding how much of previous hidden state to retain, 
while the \textbf{reset gate} determines how much of previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to forget and how much of the new
input $x_t$ to consider when computing the candidate hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$ by using a convex between the two.
The GRU equations are as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        z_t &= \sigma(W_z \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(update gate)}\\
        r_t &= \sigma(W_r \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(reset gate)}\\
        \tilde{h}_t &= \tanh(W_h \cdot [r_t \odot h_{t-1}, x_t]) \;\; \text{(candidate hidden state)}\\
        h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t \;\; \text{(hidden state update)}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\section{Deep Recurrent Neural Networks}
The concept of \textit{depth} on RNNs is ambiguous and can be extended in multiple ways, as discussed by \cite{pascanu2014constructdeeprecurrentneural}.
Firstly, we say that a deep network applies multiple non-linear transformations to the input data; this is trivial for FNNs,
but in the case of vanilla \footnote{In literature "vanilla" term refers to non-gated RNNs as the equation \ref{eq:rnn} describes} RNNs, the depth is embedded into the temporal structure, since we apply at each time step the same non-linearity.
In the picture \ref{fig:rnn_depths} we can see that there are several ways to introduce depth in RNNs.\newline
If we implement the depth from the \textit{input-to-hidden} layers, the networks improve at disentangling the underlying factors of variations
from the original input signal, similar to how Convolutional layers in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) extract features hierarchically \cite{zeiler2013visualizingunderstandingconvolutionalnetworks}.
For \textit{hidden-to-output} intermediate layers instead, the networks learn a more compact latent representation, resulting in a more efficient summarization of input history into the hidden state that 
leads to the output layer, so the readout is simplified.\newline
Yet another way to introduce depth is the \textit{hidden-to-hidden} transition, which essentially allows the hidden state to adapt to mode shifts in the input signal rapidly, this could be particularly useful in non-stationary environments, however,
a deep transition approach could lead to optimization issues due to the increased depth in the temporal dimension.
\newline
Finally, we can stack multiple recurrent layers on top of each other, which is the main drive of this thesis, this approach encourages the model to learn at different timescales
with the first layers capturing fast dynamics and the deeper layers capturing slower ones.

\section{Reservoir Computing}
Reservoir Computing (RC) is a computational framework introduced in the early 2000s with two key models: Echo State Networks (ESN) \cite{jaeger2001echo} and
Liquid State Machines (LSM) \cite{fernando2003pattern}. Both approaches aim to simplify the training of RNNs while exploiting the inherent dynamical properties of real-world physical systems to
obtain a \textit{"reservoir"} of rich dynamics that can be leveraged to solve complex tasks. 
A major advantage of RC lies in its efficiency: only the linear readout layer is trained, while recurrent connections remain fixed.
\newline
The central idea is to avoid training the recurrent connection in the network by using a fixed high-dimensional reservoir that projects the input signal into a high-dimensional feature space,
where a linear classifier is sufficient to learn the linearized representation of the input signal. \newline
Reservoir Computing has been successfully applied to tasks such as time series prediction, speech recognition, robot control \todo{add citation here}, furthermore can also be extended to generative settings using techniques such as Teacher Forcing \cite{6795228}.\newline
\newline
The focus of this work is on RC methods that address the challenge of solving learning long-term dependencies, by directly avoiding
the use of BPTT and its issues. From a theoretical viewpoint, RC models can be viewed as generalization of \textbf{kernel methods} \cite[Ch. 5]{haykin2009neural} to sequential data,
 these type of models solve the task in two steps: firstly they project the input data into a high dimensional space using a fixed non-linear mapping (the reservoir in RC),
  secondly they learn a linear model on top of this representation to make predictions.
 \newline
Both RC and kernel methods relies on the \textbf{Cover's Theorem} \cite{cover2006geometrical}, which states :
\begin{quote}
    \label{quote:cover}
    \textit{"A complex pattern-classification problem, cast in a high-dimensional space nonlinearly, is more likely to be linearly separable than in a low-dimensional space."}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Echo State Networks}
\label{sec:esn}
ESNs are a specific type of RNNs, where only a small part of recurrent connections in the hidden layer is active, creating a sparse connectivity pattern.
A common approach to build rich reservoirs \cite[Ch 10.8]{lecun2015deep} is to design the recurrence within a state-space system that operates in the \textit{edge of chaos} regime as described by \cite{NIPS2004_f8da71e5} \cite{LANGTON1986120}.
 The idea is to constrain the eigenvalues of the system's Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}$ to be close to $1$. As we previously mentioned in sec \ref{sec:rnn_long_term_dependencies},
 we want to enforce this condition on: $\mathbf{J}_t = \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}}$.
 \newline
 In practice, this is controlled by the \textbf{spectral radius} of such $\mathbf{J}$, defined as the largest absolute eigenvalue of the matrix, denoted as $\rho(\mathbf{J}) = \max{|\lambda_i|}$.
 By constraining $\rho(\mathbf{J})$ to be close to $1$, we can ensure that the system is in the edge of chaos regime, which is characterized by a balance between stability and instability.
 \newline
 In ESNs we can control the spectral radius of the recurrent weight matrix $W_{rec} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_r \times N_r}$ directly during initialization by scaling it appropriately.
 Given a randomly initialized weight matrix $W_{rec}^{init}$, we can compute its spectral radius $\rho(W_{rec}^{init})$ and then scale it to a desired value $\rho_{desired}$:
 \begin{equation}
    W_{rec} = \frac{\rho_{desired}}{\rho(W_{rec}^{init})} W_{rec}^{init}
 \end{equation}
The input weight matrix $W_{in} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_r \times N_u}$ is usually scaled by an hyperparameter called \textbf{input scaling} $\omega_{in}$, 
which controls how the input signal influences the reservoir dynamics to be in a linear or non-linear regime for small or large input magnitudes respectively.
This parameter is crucial because it affects how the reservoir responds to different input magnitudes and can impact the overall performance of the ESN on various tasks.
We scale $W_{in}$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
    W_{in} = \omega_{in} W_{in}^{init} \\
    % or norm 2
\end{equation}
 % ripetitivo
 %By choosing $\rho_{desired}$ close to $1$, we are promoting rich dynamics that are sensitive to input variations while still being stable enough to retain information over time.

A more general formulation is the Leaky Integrator ESN is given by \cite{JAEGER2007335}, which introduces a leaking rate $\alpha \in ]0,1]$ to control the response speed of the reservoir dynamics:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}(t) = (1 - \alpha) \mathbf{h}(t-1) + \alpha \tanh(W_{in}\mathbf{u}(t) + W_{rec}\mathbf{h}(t-1) + \mathbf{b})
\end{equation}
When $\alpha = 1$, the Leaky ESN reduces to the standard ESN, while for $\alpha < 1$, the reservoir state updates more slowly, behaving as a low-pass filter, thus allowing it to capture longer-term dependencies in the input sequence.
\subsubsection{Tikhonov Regularization}
 Once the reservoir is initialized, we can use it to process input sequences and collect the reservoir states over time.
After we computed the reservoir states $\mathbf{h}(t)$ for the entire input sequence, we have to find $W_{out}$ that maps the reservoir states to the desired output.
 
Given a set of target outputs $\mathbf{y}(t)$ corresponding to each input $\mathbf{x}(t)$, our objective is to minimize the squared norm of the difference between the actual outputs and the predicted outputs from the reservoir states:

 \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} ||\mathbf{y}(t) - W_{out} \mathbf{h}(t)||^2
\end{equation}

We can train the output weights $W_{out}$ using ridge regression in closed form as:
\begin{equation}
    W_{out} = Y H^T (H H^T + \lambda I)^{-1}
\end{equation}
Where $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N_y \times T}$ is the matrix of target outputs, $H \in \mathbb{R}^{N_r \times T}$ is the matrix of reservoir states collected over time, $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter, and $I$ is the identity matrix.\newline

To prevent overfitting on readout layer, and since the matrix $H$ is often ill-conditioned, we use regularization hyperparameter $\lambda$ \cite{10.2307/1271436} during the training of the output weights.
What this does is essentially to keep the diagonal of the matrix $H H^T$ away from zero, improving its condition number and making the inversion numerically stable.
\subsubsection{Transients dynamics}
It is also possible that random effects from the initialization of the reservoir can lead to suboptimal performance; hence, usually the initial timesteps collected by the reservoir are discarded,
because the reservoir undergoes a \textit{transient dynamics} phase before the state becomes fully dependent on input history rather than initial conditions.
In literature, this phase is called \textbf{Washout period} $T_{\text{washout}}$.
Given the corresponding reservoir states $\{\mathbf{h}(t)\}_{t=1}^{T}$, only the states $\{\mathbf{h}(t)\}_{t=T_{\text{washout}}+1}^{T}$ 
are used for training the readout layer, the washout period $T_{\text{washout}}$ is empirically determined, usually hundreds or thousands of timesteps are sufficient.

\subsection{Separation Property}
Besides stability, a useful reservoir should also be able to \textit{distinguish} different input histories.
In the Reservoir Computing literature (especially for Liquid State Machines), this intuition is captured by the \textbf{separation property} (also called \textbf{pointwise separation}) \cite{maass2002realtime}.
Informally, the reservoir should map different input streams to different internal states, so that a linear readout can discriminate them.
More precisely, for two bounded input sequences $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)$ that differ at least at one time step, the corresponding reservoir state trajectories $\mathbf{h}(t)$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(t)$ should become different at some time:
\begin{equation}
    \exists\, t \ge 0 \;:\; \mathbf{h}(t) \neq \tilde{\mathbf{h}}(t)
\end{equation}
This property is closely related to the common RC intuition that the reservoir acts as a high-dimensional non-linear expansion of the input (cf. \ref{quote:cover}), while the readout performs the final linear separation.

\subsection{Echo State Property}
While separation ensures that different inputs can lead to different internal representations, RC models also require the reservoir dynamics to be \textit{stable} so that states do not depend on arbitrary initial conditions.
The \textbf{Echo State Property} (ESP) \cite{jaeger2001echo} formalizes this requirement, stating that for any bounded input sequence,
 the reservoir state should asymptotically depend only on the input history and not on the initial state.\newline
 Formally, let $\mathbf{x}(t)$ be a bounded input sequence and $\mathbf{h}_1(0)$, $\mathbf{h}_2(0)$ be two different initial reservoir states
 obtained by running that sequence through the reservoir. The ESP requires that:
 \begin{equation}
    \label{ESP}
    \lim_{t \to \infty} ||\mathbf{h}_1(t) - \mathbf{h}_2(t)|| \to 0 
 \end{equation}
When this condition holds, the model behaves as a \textit{fading memory} filter, where the influence of past inputs diminishes over time, in DS theory
these are also called \textit{contractive systems} \cite{strogatz:2000}. 
\begin{definition}
    A function $f: \mathbb{R}^N \times \mathbb{R}^M \to \mathbb{R}^N$ is \textbf{contractive} if there exists a constant $0 \leq k < 1$ such that for all $\mathbf{h}_1, \mathbf{h}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^M$:
    \begin{equation}
        ||f(\mathbf{h}_1, \mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{h}_2, \mathbf{x})|| \leq k ||\mathbf{h}_1 - \mathbf{h}_2||
    \end{equation}  
    And $k$ is called the \textbf{Lipschitz constant} of the function $f$.
\end{definition}
\newline
For it to be true, we must impose two conditions, a necessary and a sufficient one, both regarding the spectral properties of the recurrent weight matrix $W_{rec}$.
The necessary condition requires:
\begin{equation}
    ||\rho(W_{rec})|| < 1
\end{equation}
While the sufficient condition requires:
\begin{equation}
    ||\sigma_{max}(W_{rec})|| < 1
\end{equation}
Where $\rho(W_{rec})$ is the spectral radius of the matrix $W_{rec}$
and $\sigma_{max}(W_{rec})$ is the largest singular value of the matrix $W_{rec}$, recall from \cite{0521386322}[pp. 347], that
the spectral radius is always upper bounded by the largest singular value: $\rho(W_{rec}) \leq \sigma_{max}(W_{rec}) = ||W_{rec}||_2$.
\newline
Although ESP guarantees that the reservoir state depends uniquely on the input history and not on initial conditions, enforcing its sufficient condition,
$\rho(\mathbf{W}_{\text{rec}}) < 1$, is often too restrictive in practice.
This bound ensures that the recurrent dynamics are contractive even in the absence of input, thereby guaranteeing stability under all circumstances. 
However, it also leads to reservoirs with overly damped dynamics, where perturbations decay too quickly, and the network loses its ability to store temporal information.
The sufficient conditions are derived under the assumption of an autonomous system, neglecting the stabilizing effect of the continuous external input.
Empirically, the most effective reservoirs operate near the edge of chaos, and the spectral radius is often set even slightly above $1$ in some cases \cite{cucchi2022hands}.

\section{Deep Echo State Networks}
In the research field of Reservoir Computing, the concept of depth has been explored to enhance the representational capacity of reservoirs.
By stacking multiple recurrent layers, the models can capture hierarchical temporal features at different timescales \cite{pascanu2014constructdeeprecurrentneural},
and improve performance on complex tasks requiring multi-scale processing or longer memory retention.
\newline
In \cite{GALLICCHIO201787} \cite{gallicchio2017echo} the authors focus on the analysis of Deep Echo State Networks (DeepESN). 
Having multiple sequentially stacked layers where information can be processed, allows for a different hidden state for each of them. The signal is given to the first sub-reservoir and then
propagated through the stack, altering intrinsic dynamics and allowing a more expressive architectural bias.
Experiments have also shown an increase in memory capability, thus DeepESNs are able to retain more information about past inputs compared to shallow ESNs with the same number of total neurons.

We define a Deep Echo State Network (DeepESN), with leaky integrator neurons, as a stack of $L$ recurrent reservoir layers, where each layer $l$ has its own state vector $\mathbf{h}^{(l)}(t)$.
Each layer can have a different leaking rate, input scaling (from the previous layer) and spectral radius.
The input to the first layer is the external input $\mathbf{x}(t)$, while the input to subsequent layers is the state of the previous layer.
\newline
The state update equations for a DeepESN are given by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}^{(l)}(t) = (1 - \alpha^{(l)}) \mathbf{h}^{(l)}(t-1) + \alpha^{(l)} \tanh(W_{in}^{(l)} \mathbf{x}(t) + W_{rec}^{(l)} \mathbf{h}^{(l)}(t-1) + \mathbf{b}^{(l)})
\end{equation}
With $W_{in}^{(1)} \in\ \mathbb{R}^\mathbf{N_r \times N_u}$, when $l\neq1$ $W_{in}^{(l)} \in\ \mathbb{R}^\mathbf{N_r \times N_r}$ 
the input weights matrix from previous layer, $\alpha^{(l)} \in [0,1]$ the leaking rate,
 $W_{rec}^{(l)} \in\ \mathbb{R}^\mathbf{N_r \times N_r}$ the recurrent weights matrix, $\mathbf{b}^{(l)} \in\ \mathbb{R}^\mathbf{N_r}$ the bias vector.
 
For the readout phase, we can use the concatenated states from all layers or just the last layer:

% Open big {} and put the concatenated or last layer state as a system of equation in a single begin equation
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
    \mathbf{y}(t) = W_{out} [\mathbf{h}^{(1)}(t); \mathbf{h}^{(2)}(t); \ldots; \mathbf{h}^{(L)}(t)] & \text{(concatenated states)} \\
    \mathbf{y}(t) = W_{out} \mathbf{h}^{(L)}(t) & \text{(last layer state only)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Echo state property of DeepESN}
\label{sec:deepesn_esp}
ESNs are designed to satisfy the Echo State Property (ESP) \cite{jaeger2001echo}, which ensures that the reservoir's state is uniquely determined by the input history.
When extending ESNs to DeepESNs, it is crucial to analyse how the ESP is affected by the depth of the network.
In \cite{gallicchio2017echo}, the authors provide sufficient and necessary conditions for the ESP to hold in DeepESNs.
They show that the ESP can be guaranteed if each layer of such a model satisfies the ESP individually, and if the inter-layer connections are appropriately scaled.
Specifically, they derive conditions on the spectral radius of the recurrent weight matrices and the norms of the inter-layer weight matrices to ensure stability and the ESP.
\newline
Formally, for a DeepESN with $L$ layers, where each layer $l$ is governed by a leaking rate $a^{(l)}$, a recurrent weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$, and receives input from the previous layer via $\mathbf{W}_{in}^{(l)}$, the conditions are defined as follows.

\paragraph{Necessary Condition}
The necessary condition for the asymptotic stability of the zero state (and thus for the ESP) relies on the analysis of the system's Jacobian. 
Due to the hierarchical, feedforward architecture of a standard DeepESN, the Jacobian is a block lower triangular matrix.
 Consequently, its global spectral radius is determined by the maximum of the spectral radii of the individual layers.
  The condition is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \rho(\mathbf{J}_{DeepESN, x}(0_u, 0)
     = \max_{l=1,\dots,L} \left( \rho \left( (1-a^{(l)})\mathbf{I} + a^{(l)}\mathbf{W_{rec}}^{(l)} \right) \right) < 1,
\end{equation}
where $\rho(\cdot)$ denotes the spectral radius and $\mathbf{I}$, $0_u$ is the zero input, and $0$ is the zero state of the DeepESN.
 is the identity matrix. This implies that for the network to be stable around 
 the zero state, every layer must individually satisfy the local stability criterion.

\paragraph{Sufficient Condition}
The sufficient condition requires the global dynamics of the DeepESN to be contractive. This is ensured if the maximum Lipschitz constant among all layers, denoted as $C^{(l)}$, is less than 1. The contraction coefficients are defined recursively to account for the propagation of signals through the depth of the network:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        C^{(1)} = (1-a^{(1)}) + a^{(1)} \|\mathbf{W_{rec}}^{(1)}\| \\
        C^{(l)} = (1-a^{(l)}) + a^{(l)} \left( C^{(l-1)} \|\mathbf{W}_{in}^{(l)}\| + \|\mathbf{W_{rec}}^{(l)}\| \right) & \text{for } l > 1,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the induced matrix norm (e.g., the spectral norm). The ESP is guaranteed if:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{l=1,\dots,L} C^{(l)} < 1.
\end{equation}
This indicates that in deeper layers, the contractivity depends not only on the local recurrent weights $\mathbf{W_{rec}}^{(l)}$ but also on the contraction of the previous layer $C^{(l-1)}$ and the magnitude of the inter-layer weights $\mathbf{W}_{in}^{(l)}$.
\newline
Because the recursive term involves $C^{(l-1)}$, the contraction coefficient increases with depth, making deeper layers less contractive and able to retain information longer.

\section{Antisymmetric RNNs}
\label{sec:antisymmetric_rnn}

Another approach to mitigating the vanishing and exploding gradient problem in recurrent neural networks is the
\textit{AntisymmetricRNN} proposed by \cite{chang2019antisymmetricrnndynamicalviewrecurrent}.\newline
The method is based on interpreting RNNs as continuous-time dynamical systems and designing the recurrent weight
matrix according to stability principles from ODE theory.
\newline
The core idea is to constrain the recurrent matrix to be \textbf{antisymmetric}, because antisymmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues.
\begin{equation}
    W_{rec} = - W_{rec}^T
\end{equation}
This property ensures that the continuous-time system is \textbf{stable}, meaning that small perturbations in the hidden state do not grow unbounded over time.
\newline
Since only being stable is not sufficient to capture long-term dependencies, the authors argue that if the system is dissipative,
then using such an ODE would result in catastrophic forgetting of past inputs in the forward pass.
A possible solution is to design a system that operates in a critical regime, such idea is formalized through the following \textbf{critical criterion}:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{i} \operatorname{Re}\!\left( \lambda_i(J(t)) \right) \approx 0, 
    \qquad \forall\, t > 0,
\end{equation}
meaning that the real parts of the Jacobian eigenvalues remain close to zero.
In this regime, long-term dependencies are preserved without inducing instability.\newline
The proposed ODE system is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    h'(t) = \tanh((W_{rec} - W_{rec}^T)h(t) + W_{in}x(t) + b)
\end{equation}
\newline
If we derive the Jacobian of the system, we obtain:
\begin{equation}
    J(t) = \operatorname{diag}[{\tanh'((W_{rec} - W_{rec}^T)h(t) + W_{in}x(t) + b)}](W_{rec} - W_{rec}^T)
\end{equation}
Since the eigenvalues of an antisymmetric matrix are purely imaginary, and the derivative of the $\tanh$ activation is bounded between $0$ and $1$,
the eigenvalues of the Jacobian $J(t)$ will have real parts close to zero, satisfying the critical criterion.
\newline\newline
This condition for a discretized version of the ODE is very similar to the edge of chaos condition discussed previously for ESNs in sec \ref{sec:esn}, where the spectral radius of the Jacobian is constrained to be close to $1$.
To ensure stability after discretization with forward Euler method with timestep $\epsilon$, the authors introduce a small diffusion term
$-\gamma I$, since the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix: $|1+ \epsilon\lambda_i(J_t)| > 1$, leading to the following update equation for the hidden state:
\begin{equation}
    h(t) = h(t-1) + \epsilon tanh((W_{rec}-W_{rec}^T-\gamma I)h(t-1) + W_{in}x(t)+b_h)
\end{equation}

\section{Random Oscillator Networks}
Random Oscillator Networks (RONs) \cite{pmlr-v238-ceni24a} are a class of physically-inspired reservoir computing models that drive the input signal through
a network of heterogeneous oscillators, this model for chaotic time series prediction tasks outperformed other randomized approaches on long-term memory for sequence classification.
The idea behind RONs is to leverage the rich dynamics of coupled oscillators to create a reservoir that, even on a small parameterization regime, can achieve high performance on temporal tasks.
\newline
\subsection*{Physical Dissipation and Fading Memory}
 In RONs, we get the \textit{fading memory} property in the energy dissipation of damped oscillators. 
 Considering the continuous-time energy function of a single unforced oscillator $\mathcal{E}(t) = \frac{1}{2}\dot{y}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\gamma y^2$, its time derivative is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathcal{E}}(t) = -\epsilon \dot{y}^2.
\end{equation}
For a strictly positive damping coefficient $\epsilon > 0$, the energy is strictly decreasing ($\dot{\mathcal{E}} \leq 0$), ensuring that the system asymptotically forgets its initial state transient.
\newline\newline
The RON is defined as a discretized system of coupled Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs). Let $\mathbf{y}_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and $\mathbf{z}_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$ represent the position and velocity vectors of the $N$ oscillators at time step $k$. 
The state update is governed by explicit Euler discretization with step size $\tau$:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:RON_update}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{y}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{y}_k + \tau \mathbf{z}_{k+1}, \\
    \mathbf{z}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{z}_k + \tau \left[ \tanh(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}_k + \mathbf{V}\mathbf{u}_{k+1} + \mathbf{b}) - \bm{\gamma} \odot \mathbf{y}_k - \bm{\epsilon} \odot \mathbf{z}_k \right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\bm{\gamma}, \bm{\epsilon} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ are the heterogeneous stiffness and damping coefficients, respectively, and $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product. The term $\tanh(\cdot)$ acts as a non-linear forcing function, coupling the oscillators through the recurrent weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$.

\subsection*{Jacobian and Stability Analysis}
To analyze the stability and the spectral properties of the reservoir, we consider the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_k$ of the state mapping $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{y}_k, \mathbf{z}_k)$. By linearizing Eq. (\ref{eq:RON_update}), the Jacobian exhibits a specific block structure:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:RON_Jacobian}
\mathbf{J}_k = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{\partial \mathbf{y}_{k+1}}{\partial \mathbf{y}_k} & \frac{\partial \mathbf{y}_{k+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_k} \\[6pt]
    \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_{k+1}}{\partial \mathbf{y}_k} & \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_{k+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_k}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{I} + \tau^2 \mathbf{A}_k & \tau(\mathbf{I} - \tau \text{diag}(\bm{\epsilon})) \\[6pt]
    \tau \mathbf{A}_k & \mathbf{I} - \tau \text{diag}(\bm{\epsilon})
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{A}_k = \mathbf{S}_k \mathbf{W} - \text{diag}(\bm{\gamma})$, and $\mathbf{S}_k$
 is the diagonal matrix of derivatives of the activation function, 
 $\mathbf{S}_k = \text{diag}(\mathbf{1} - \tanh^2(\dots))$.

This block structure reveals that the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_k$ are clustered in disks centered
 at $1 - \tau \epsilon$ and $1 - \tau^2 \gamma$.\newline
  By tuning these hyperparameters, we can constrain the spectrum within
  the unit circle, facilitating the \textit{edge of stability} regime required for long-term memory tasks.
  For small values of $\tau$, the eigenvalues are close to $1$, promoting slow decay of information and enhancing memory retention.
